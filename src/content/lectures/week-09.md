Half and half, like every lecture: George Curious on why building with
someone is a different transaction from everything you've done so far,
Moana Liza — Crossing 5's tutor — on how you actually do it with whatever
is lying around, and what you owe the room afterwards.

## George's half — why build with someone

Four Crossings in, you have met four kinds of stranger: someone you said
something to, someone you traded with, someone who walked up to a stall
your team had built, and someone who could have said no to you and maybe
did. In every one of them the other person was on the far side of the
exchange. [Find a Partner in the Hall](/sessions/09-find-a-partner/) puts
them on your side of it, which is a harder thing to arrange and a much
harder thing to fake.

The failure mode has a shape, and George names it early: you agree on a
thing, you each go and do a piece, you staple the pieces together and call
it a build. Sawyer and DeZutter's study of improvised theatre is the
framework for why that isn't the same object — they analyse a creative
process in which no single participant's contribution determines the
result, and list what a group needs for that to happen. An outcome nobody
has scripted. Each move depending on the one just before it. Anyone's later
move able to change what an earlier one meant. Split the work in two and
you have removed all three on purpose.

So the price of this Crossing isn't nerve, or an object, or a no. It's sole
authorship: you give up the right to decide how the thing turns out, to
somebody whose name you learned that morning. And because two people made
it, this is the first Crossing where the account of it isn't only yours
either — [What We Made](/sessions/10-what-we-made/) is a report with
someone else's decisions in it, and George closes their half on the test that
follows from that. Not "was it worth doing" this time, but: would your
partner recognise the thing you just described?

## Moana's half — how you actually do it

Moana teaches Crossing 5 the way she teaches everything, which is to look
at the bench before looking for an idea. Cardboard, wire, a broken umbrella
someone left in the Maker Space, a common-room Lego shelf, a phone that
records: what's in reach decides what two strangers can plausibly make in
an afternoon, and deciding it the other way round is how people end up
negotiating a concept until the hallway empties.

Which is where the marshmallow comes in. Wujec has run the same eighteen
minutes — twenty sticks of spaghetti, a yard of tape, one marshmallow on
top — with tens of thousands of people, and kindergarteners reliably build
taller than business students, because the kids start putting sticks into
the marshmallow immediately and the students spend the time planning and
then have none left to fix what the plan got wrong. Build in the first five
minutes. You will be wrong faster, which is the point.

The other half of the craft is what you do with what your partner says, and
improv has had the vocabulary for it longer than we have: everything is an
offer, accepting is saying yes to the reality they've put in front of you,
and blocking is rejecting it. Johnstone's line is that good improvisers look
telepathic because they accept all offers made — take the offer and you get
a build; block it and you get your own idea with a witness. Moana closes on
what leaves the hallway with you: the fifth and final clip, shot under the
rules on [Policies](/policies/) if your partner is in it, and a report next
week that covers a decision that wasn't yours.

## What you should leave knowing

- why a thing built together is a different object from two halves stapled
  together, and what a marker can see in the difference
- what this Crossing costs you that the first four didn't
- how to start building before you've agreed what you're building
- what to do with an offer you wouldn't have made yourself
- what you owe the room when the story has two people in it
