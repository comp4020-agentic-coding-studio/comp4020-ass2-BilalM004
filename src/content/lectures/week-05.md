One presenter this fortnight rather than the usual two: George Curious is
both the convenor and Crossing 3's tutor, so the handover in the middle is
just a change of register. Same shape as every other week — the idea
first, then how to actually do it.

## The first half — why a team changes the question

Crossings 1 and 2 both worked because you were standing there starting
them. Crossing 3 assigns you four other people and asks for something that
doesn't depend on that, which is a different problem rather than a larger
version of the same one.

The evidence half starts with Ringelmann, who hooked a rope to a pressure
gauge and had people pull: add pullers and the total climbs, but each
person's share of it falls. Latané, Williams and Harkins ran the same shape
of experiment with clapping and shouting and separated the two reasons a
group underperforms — some of the loss is bad coordination, and some of it
is simply effort. The second kind is the one you can do something about,
because it shrinks when each person's output is measurable. It was never
laziness, it was being un-measurable. So the accountability has to come
from inside the team: distinct jobs, named out loud, before the table goes
up.

The half closes on the test the whole Crossing turns on. If the thing stops
being a thing the moment nobody is standing next to it, you built a
conversation with extra steps.

## The second half — how to actually run one

Whyte's word for what makes a stranger stop is triangulation: some external
stimulus that prompts strangers to talk to strangers as if they knew each
other. His example, via Project for Public Spaces, is a bench, a bin and a
phone — scattered, each gets very little use; put together with a coffee
cart, people arrive. Nobody stops to talk to a table. They stop for the
thing on it, and [Set Up Shop](/sessions/05-set-up-shop/) asks your stall to
*be* that thing — including across the lunch break, when it is one of you
holding the table rather than five.

Then the telling, which is where the Crossing is actually won. Week 6 is
one slot per team in a fuller room — the [Hallway
Showcase](/sessions/06-hallway-showcase/) — and the shape of the account is
the army's after-action review, more or less: what was supposed to happen,
what actually happened, why there was a difference, what you've learned.
Most teams arrive able to do the first. Almost nobody arrives with the
third. Running those four questions against the stall you're *planning* is
the most useful hour of the fortnight, which is why the framework is in
week 5 and not week 6.

It ends on the one failure mode George can always spot: an account
assembled for a bigger audience, rather than the one you were giving each
other while it was still going wrong.

## What you should leave knowing

- why five of you is not five times one of you, and what stops it being
  worse
- what "bigger" is actually asking for — exposure and hours, not flash
- the test every stall has to pass: it survives all five of you walking
  away
- four questions the Showcase account has to answer, and which two you'll
  be short on
