Half and half, like every lecture: George Curious on why an ask you might
not get is worth your week, Dora De Exploré — Crossing 4's tutor — on how
to actually make one and what you owe the room afterwards.

## George's half — the price of not asking

First lecture back, and the break has taken the edge off whatever nerve
Crossings 1 to 3 built. It also removes the thing that made Crossing 3
easier than it looked: Set Up Shop gave you a team and a stall to stand
behind. Crossing 4 gives you neither.

The risk also moves. Up to now you spent something you already had —
attention, a thing worth trading, a table and some hours. In
[Ask Anyway](/sessions/07-ask-anyway/) you ask for something you don't
have, from someone entitled to refuse, and there's no scale bar this time
because the risk is sitting inside the sentence you say out loud.

Then the finding the Crossing is built on. Flynn and Lake sent people out
to make direct requests for help of strangers, after asking them to
predict how many would agree — and across six studies, in the lab and in
the field, people were out by as much as half. The reason is the good
part: an asker prices up how much trouble a yes would be, and forgets
entirely how awkward a no is to say. George closes on the asymmetry that
follows. A no you can report; the ask you talked yourself out of leaves no
trace at all — which is why the Crossing asks you to name what you'd have
lost by staying quiet. Jia Jiang, who went looking for rejection once a
day for a hundred days, is the existence proof that the sting fades faster
than anyone predicts.

## Dora's half — ask, then account for it

Dora has been told no in more languages than she speaks, over a lift out
of a town with no bus timetable, a spare tent peg, a night train seat she
had no ticket for. She's still here, so it's survivable, and her half is
the practical version of that: decide the ask in the moment rather than
the night before, say it out loud to the person in front of you, and if
it's a no, adjust the ask and go again.

Out loud is not a stylistic preference. Bohns and Roghanizad had people
run the same request two ways, face to face and by email; the askers
expected both to work about as well, and the in-person version came in 34
times more effective. So the quest is the pretext and the ask is the
Crossing — no texting it, no sending a friend.

The other half is the telling, and Dora spends the back of her half there.
[The Rejection Report](/sessions/08-the-rejection-report/) wants the ask
before the answer, and a yes gets exactly the same honest account as a no,
because the room is asking what you risked rather than whether it worked
out — see [Crossing Participation](/assessments/crit-participation/) for
how that's marked. The clip is the fourth of your five, and a no is as
worth thirty seconds as a yes; if the person you asked is in it, the order
of operations is on [Policies](/policies/).

## What you should leave knowing

- that strangers agree far more often than askers predict, and why the
  prediction is wrong in that direction
- why the ask has to be spoken, in person, to count
- what you'd have lost by not asking — the question you'll be answering
  next week
- that a no is a report, and a skipped ask is nothing at all
