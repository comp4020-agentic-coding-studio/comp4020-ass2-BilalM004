You stood there. You started it. If it worked, it worked because you were

## You didn't pick this team

Four other people, assigned for this Crossing, for two weeks. Nobody asked
you who you'd have chosen.

That's not an oversight. A team you choose is a team you already know how
to work with, and knowing how to work with people you didn't choose is
most of what a hallway actually asks of you.

## Five of you is not five times one of you

Ringelmann hooked a rope to a pressure gauge and had people pull. Add
pullers and the total climbs. Each person's share of it falls.

Latané ran it again with clapping and shouting, and split the loss in two:
some of it is bad coordination, and some of it is just effort.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Don't quote a figure. The 1913 numbers circulate widely but the paper on
the reading list reports the split, not those, and I'm not citing something
I haven't read. The effect is the point, and the next slide is the
interesting half of it.
```

---

## Why it happens, and what fixes it

Effort drops when nobody can tell whose effort it was. Set people shouting
in a group, measure each person's output separately, and the effect
disappears — it was never laziness, it was being un-measurable.

So: distinct jobs, named out loud, before the table goes up. Not because I
want a list. Because "we all did a bit of everything" is the exact
condition the effect needs.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Be straight with them about the awkward bit: the Checkpoint deliberately
marks the stall as one thing, which is the un-measurable condition. I'm
not going to fix that by marking slices — the point of the Crossing is the
stall. So the accountability has to come from inside the team, and this
slide is how.
```

---

## Bigger isn't flashier

More exposure. More hours it has to stay up. More people who have to
actually turn up for it to exist at all.

More that can go wrong is the feature, not the risk you're supposed to
engineer out.

# The test is what happens when all five of you walk away.

If it stops being a thing the moment nobody's standing next to it, you
built a conversation with extra steps.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

This is the line I want them arguing about on the way out. Every team's

It's also where my half lands: scale isn't the point, it's the only way to
find out what you can make happen that you can't personally stand next to.
Two weeks, one stall, one question — was it worth building.

> Triangulation is the process by which some external stimulus provides a
> linkage between people and prompts strangers to talk to other strangers
> as if they knew each other.
>
> <cite>William H. Whyte, quoted in Project for Public Spaces, [Eleven
> Principles for Creating Great Community

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Whyte spent years filming plazas to work out why some of them were full and
the identical one next door was empty. Triangulation was his answer, and
it's the closest thing this Crossing has to a method.
```

## Put a third thing between you

A bench, a bin and a phone, scattered, each get very little use. Put them
together with a coffee cart and people arrive.

Nobody stops to talk to a table. They stop for the thing on it — the
absurd object, the question they can answer in one word, the board that's
already got four answers on it and needs a fifth.

Your stall's job is to be the third thing.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
The bench/bin/phone example is Whyte's, via PPS, and it's worth saying out
loud that it's about arrangement rather than budget. Cheap triangulates
fine.
```

---

## Build the part that does the inviting

A sign has to work when none of you are there to explain it. So does a
chalk line on the floor, a jar with a slot, an arrow.

Write it for the person who is already walking past and did not slow down.
If it needs you standing beside it to make sense, it isn't done.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Push on lunch cover here. Somebody has to hold the table while the rest of
the team eats, and the teams that don't plan for that find out on the day.
```

## Then all five of you stand at the front

Week 6 isn't the usual circle. One slot per team, a fuller room than
you're used to, and the whole team up there for it —
[Hallway Showcase](/sessions/06-hallway-showcase/).

Building it in the hallway is half. This is the other half — one account
between five of you, and questions aimed at the stall rather than at any
one of you. "That was someone else's part" isn't an answer up there.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Flag the room size early. Teams that hear "bigger audience" for the first
time on the day present to the wall.
```

---

## Four questions your account has to answer

The after-action review, more or less as the army runs it: what was
supposed to happen, what actually happened, why there was a difference,
and what you've learned.

Questions two and three are where the Showcase actually lives. Every team
turns up able to answer question one. Almost nobody turns up with three.

What that presentation has to contain is on
[Midsemester Checkpoint](/assessments/midsem-checkpoint/).

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Get them running these four on the stall they're *planning*, tonight. It
changes what gets built, which is the entire reason the framework is in
week 5 and not week 6.

Warn them how I ask: at the stall, not at a person, and I keep going until
the team answers as a team. If four of them look at one of them, I've
learned something about the stall.
```

# Tidy is the failure mode.

The account that matches what you told each other while it was happening
beats the one you assembled for a bigger room.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Last thing they hear from me before they go and build it. A polished
version of a fortnight that wasn't polished is the one thing I can always
spot, and it costs more than whatever went wrong did.
```
