## A boring report-back isn't a bad grade waiting to happen

It's a sign the trade needed another pass.

You can't narrate your way out of an interaction where nothing was at
stake. So stop fixing the telling, and fix the thing you're telling
about.

```notes

"how to make a trade worth telling" or "how to tell it".
```

## No cash, on purpose

Money doesn't price a social exchange. It ends one.

Dan Ariely's version: people will happily help you move a couch for
nothing, and refuse to help you move it for three dollars. Introduce a
price and the favour stops being a favour — it becomes a bad job offer.

Every trade this week runs on the other set of rules.

```notes
The couch example lands better than the abstract version. If they push
back, the daycare late-fine study is the other one — fining late parents
made lateness *more* common, because a fine is a price and a price is
permission.
```

---

## Name your price first

You say what you're offering before you know what's coming back. That's the
whole risk, and the spec checks you can still say what you expected.

Mauss, writing in 1925 about gift economies, put the obligation in three
parts: to give, to receive, and to repay. Only the first one is yours to
control.

```notes
The reason "name it first" matters: if you wait to see what they'll offer,
you haven't made a trade, you've run an auction. And an auction is a
boring clip.
```

## Pick your currency

**Object.** A badge, a book, half a sandwich.

**Favour.** Carrying a busker's amp between sets.

**Tip.** Something you know about this campus that they don't.

**Skill.** Proofreading, a language, fixing a bike chain.

The currency matters less than whether you'd have handed it over if nobody
were marking you.

## A bad trade is still a good report

"Fair" isn't the only acceptable answer. A trade that went badly is exactly
as reportable as one that didn't — as long as you can say why.

You're marked on what the exchange bought you and whether you can account
for it, not on whether you came out ahead. See
[Crossing Participation](/assessments/crit-participation/).

```notes
Worth saying out loud: the students who get the best marks in this Crossing
are usually not the ones who got the best deal.
```

## Storyboard it

What you wanted. What you offered instead. The bit you'd cut if you only
had thirty seconds.

That's three questions I'd ask about any footage, and they're the same
three the room will ask you next week. Pixar's story spine is the long
version: *once upon a time… every day… one day… because of that…*

If you can't find the "one day" in your trade, you haven't made one yet.

```notes
This is the practical payoff. Get them to run the three questions on the
trade they're *planning*, not the one they've done — it changes what they
go and do.
```

## The clip

One per Crossing. Five by the end, and they play in week 12 as one video —
see [Legacy](/assessments/legacy/) for what the set has to do.

A clip isn't documentation. It's the thirty seconds that prove the thing
happened to somebody who wasn't there.

It's also yours, of your Crossing — not a recording of the person you traded
with. Want them in it? Ask first, record the yes, and shoot a version without
them anyway — consent can be taken back, and then you've got nothing.
[Policies](/policies/) is the short version.
