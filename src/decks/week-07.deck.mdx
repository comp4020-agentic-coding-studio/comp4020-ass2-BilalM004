## They say yes more than you think

Flynn and Lake had people predict how many strangers would agree to a
direct request for help, then sent them out to ask.

Six studies, in the lab and out in the field. The predictions came in as
much as 50% under what actually happened.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
The mechanism is the bit worth saying out loud: an asker prices up how
much trouble a yes would be, and completely forgets how awkward a no is
to say. The people being asked are pricing the second thing.

If someone wants the cite: Flynn & Lake (Bohns), 2008, six studies. The
paper says "as much as 50%" — use that phrasing, don't round it into a
single tidy number.
```

## You can count the nos

A no is a story. It has a who, a where and a sentence you actually said.

The ask you talked yourself out of has none of that. Nothing to report,
nothing to film, nothing to be asked a hard question about.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
This is a framing, not a finding — don't let it sound like it came out of
a study. The spec line it's pointing at is "you can say what you'd have
lost by not asking."
```

## A hundred days of it

Jia Jiang went looking for rejection on purpose, once a day, for a
hundred days. A loan off a stranger. A burger refill.

The yeses weren't the point. The point was that the fear got smaller each
time he tested it against a real person.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Qualitative on purpose — he publishes no effect sizes and I'm not going to
invent one. It's an existence proof, not evidence: one person, a hundred
tries, still standing.

Worth thirteen minutes of their evening. The talk is linked off the
lecture page.
```

## Most of my asks got a no

A lift out of a town with no bus timetable. A spare tent peg. The last
seat on a night train I didn't have a ticket for.

I'm still here. So we've established it's survivable.

<DeckPresenter of="dora-de-explore" />

```notes
Keep this fast and flat. The room has spent three weeks dreading this
Crossing, and a war story delivered as a shrug does more than
reassurance does.

I'm not going to pretend it doesn't sting. Only that it stops stinging
sooner than they think.
```

## Decided on the spot

Not the plan you made last night. The one you make with whoever is
actually standing there.

Go and find out whether the vending machine everyone says is broken is
broken. Settle a genuinely pointless argument. Try the food truck you've
walked past all semester.

The quest is the pretext. The ask is the Crossing.

<DeckPresenter of="dora-de-explore" />

```notes
Somebody will ask whether they can line up the quest in advance. No — and
the reason isn't rule-keeping, it's that a planned ask has all the nerve
taken out of it before you open your mouth.
```

---

## Out loud. In person.

Bohns and Roghanizad had people run one identical request two ways: half
face to face, half by email. The askers expected both to work about as
well.

Face to face came in 34 times more effective.

So: no texting it, no DM, no sending a friend to ask for you.

<DeckPresenter of="dora-de-explore" />

```notes
Their explanation is a failure of perspective-taking — the sender is
thinking about how reasonable their own request is, and the recipient is
looking at a suspicious message from a stranger.

The spec line is "you actually asked, out loud, and heard the answer."
This slide is why it's written that way.
```

## The clip — fourth of five

Wherever the quest went, or just you afterwards saying what the answer
was. A no is as worth thirty seconds as a yes, and it's often the better
thirty seconds.

It's your clip of your Crossing. If the person you asked ends up in it:
ask first, keep their yes on the record, and shoot a version without them
as well. [Policies](/policies/) has the order and the reason.

All five play at [Legacy](/assessments/legacy/).

<DeckPresenter of="dora-de-explore" />

```notes
Step three is the one that gets skipped every year. Consent can be taken
back, and a clip that only exists in the featured version is a clip that
can vanish in week 11.
```

## What you owe the room

[The Rejection Report](/sessions/08-the-rejection-report/): the ask
first — what you said, to whom, why that moment — then the answer,
however it landed.

A yes gets the same account as a no. The room wants to know what you
risked, not that it worked out.

That's where [the Crossing's mark](/assessments/crit-participation/) is
mostly made.

<DeckPresenter of="dora-de-explore" />

```notes
Warn them now about the one I see every year: the yes that gets reported
in four words because it went fine. It doesn't get to be four words.

The follow-up usually lands on the ask, not the answer — so come having
thought about what you'd change about how you asked.
```
