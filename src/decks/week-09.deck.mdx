## Four kinds of stranger so far

Someone you said something to. Someone you traded with. Someone who walked
up to a stall your team had built. Someone who could have said no, and
possibly did.

Every one of them was on the far side of the exchange. This one puts them
on your side of it — not someone you talked to, someone whose hands are in
the thing. Harder to arrange than any of the four, and much harder to fake.

## Two halves, stapled

You agree on a thing. You each go and do a piece. You bring the pieces
back and glue them together.

That is a perfectly good way to get something finished. It is not this
Crossing, and a marker can see the seam from across the room.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

---

## Where the thing actually comes from

Sawyer and DeZutter spent five improvised performances documenting a
creative process where **no single participant's contribution determines
the result**. Three things it needed:

An outcome nobody had scripted. Each move depending on the one just
before. Anyone's later move able to change what an earlier one meant.

Divide the work in two and you have removed all three, deliberately.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Their term is "distributed creativity", but I don't need the term, I need
the three conditions — they're the design of the Crossing restated as
research.
```

## What this one costs

Crossing 1 cost you nerve. Crossing 2 cost you a real thing before you
knew what was coming back. Crossing 4 cost you a no.

This one costs you sole authorship — the right to decide how it turns out,
handed to someone whose name you learned that morning.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

## And the telling isn't only yours

## Look at the bench first

Cardboard. Wire. A broken umbrella somebody abandoned in the Maker Space.
The Lego on the common-room shelf. A phone that records.

What's within reach decides what two strangers can plausibly make before
the hallway empties. Pick the idea first and you'll spend the whole
afternoon negotiating it.

<DeckPresenter of="moana-liza" />

```notes
I'll bring a box. Whatever's on the bench that morning — last time it was
a bike wheel and about forty bread tags, and someone made a — anyway,
bring the box, don't describe the box.
```

---

## Eighteen minutes, twenty sticks of spaghetti

Wujec's exercise: build the tallest thing you can, marshmallow on top.
He's run it with tens of thousands of people.

Kindergarteners beat business students, repeatedly. The kids start pushing
sticks into the marshmallow in the first minute. The students plan, then
execute the plan, then have no time left to fix what the plan got wrong.

Build in the first five minutes. You'll be wrong sooner, which is the
entire advantage.

<DeckPresenter of="moana-liza" />

---

## Take the offer

Improv has had the words for this longer than we have. Everything your
partner does is an **offer**. **Accepting** is saying yes to the reality
they've put in front of you. **Blocking** is rejecting it.

Johnstone reckoned good improvisers look telepathic, because they accept
all offers made.

Take the offer and you get a build. Block it and you get your own idea,
with a witness.

<DeckPresenter of="moana-liza" />

```notes
Two minutes of this, live: I make an offer, someone blocks it, the room
watches the thing die. Then run it again accepting. Nobody argues with it
after that.
```

## What you owe the room next week

The thing, if it travels. A description of it, if it doesn't.

Then the part that isn't yours: one decision your partner made that
changed what it became, and where the two of you disagreed. An account
that only covers your contribution is missing half of what happened —
[What We Made](/sessions/10-what-we-made/) says so plainly.

<DeckPresenter of="moana-liza" />

```notes
Tell them to write the partner's decision down on the day, in the hallway,
in whatever words they'd use standing there. A week later they'll have
tidied it into their own idea without meaning to.
```
