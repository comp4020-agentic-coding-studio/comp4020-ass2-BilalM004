## Campus is an economy

Not a metaphor you have to like — just the one this course runs on.

Attention, trust and serendipity behave like currency: finite, unevenly
distributed, worth more to some people than others, and completely wasted
if you never spend any.

<DeckPresenter of="george-curious" />

```notes
Resist explaining the metaphor too hard. It earns itself over twelve weeks;
right now it only has to be plausible enough to follow.
```

---

## So the coursework is spending it

Twelve weeks of deliberately spending attention on people you don't know,
and finding out what it buys.

That's the whole goal. Not networking, not confidence as a personality
upgrade — an actual record of what you spent and what came back.

# Talking to strangers is a protocol, not a trait.

You're not bad at this. You're running a bad forecast — and a forecast is
fixable.

<DeckPresenter of="haskell-byte" />

```notes
The claim the rest of the lecture defends. James Clear's version if they
want one: you don't rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level
of your systems. "Be more outgoing" is a goal. What follows is a system.

I did not find any of this natural. I took it apart until it worked, which
is the only qualification I have for teaching it.
```

## Part 1 — Reason

You need a reason to be standing there that isn't this course.

"Because my course told me to" isn't a reason. It's an apology with a
citation.

The person at the same library table every week. Whoever's ahead of you at
the coffee cart with nothing to do but wait. The reason can be small. It
has to be real.

<DeckPresenter of="haskell-byte" />

---

## Part 2 — Opener

Not "so, what do you do."

An opener has to survive contact: answerable by someone who wasn't
expecting you, and it has to leave them somewhere to go.

Ask about the thing in front of both of you. It's there, it's shared, and
it's evidence you were paying attention.

<DeckPresenter of="haskell-byte" />

```notes
"So, what do you do" fails both tests — it's an interview question, and the
honest answer to it is usually one word.
```

---

## Part 3 — Exit

Conversations end. Plan for it, or you'll stay ninety seconds too long and
that's the part you'll remember.

Name the thing you're leaving for. Say what you got out of it. Go.

An exit that doesn't slam the door is what makes a second conversation
possible.

<DeckPresenter of="haskell-byte" />

## What a report-back sounds like

Next week is [How It Landed](/sessions/02-how-it-landed/). Short, live, one
at a time: who you approached, what you actually said, what came back, and
whether it was worth the walk over.

The room is listening for the version you'd have given five minutes after
it happened — hesitations included. A report that's clearly been tidied up
since Monday reads as exactly that.

<DeckPresenter of="haskell-byte" />

```notes
Rehearsed is the most common failure, not shyness. People polish it because
they think polish is what's being marked. It isn't.
```

---

## Someone will ask you a follow-up

What you'd have done if they'd said no. Whether you'd approach the same way
again. What you noticed about yourself halfway through.

That isn't a stress test bolted onto the report. It's the other half of it,
and it's marked as its own criterion — see
[Crossing Participation](/assessments/crit-participation/).
